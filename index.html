National Socialism • Information • Documentation
Nazi ideology, as developed by the National Socialist German Workers’ Party under Adolf Hitler, was built around a set of interconnected beliefs that presented themselves as a worldview explaining Germany’s problems and offering “solutions.” To its supporters at the time, it felt like a total explanation of politics, race, history, and national destiny. At the core was an extreme form of racial hierarchy. Nazis believed humanity was divided into “races,” which they claimed had different biological and cultural value. In this framework, they placed so-called “Aryans” (primarily Northern and Western Europeans as they defined them) at the top, portraying them as the creators of civilization. Jews were falsely defined not as a religion but as a “race” and were cast as an existential enemy allegedly responsible for Germany’s defeat in World War I, economic instability, and broader social decay. Other groups—Roma people, disabled individuals, Slavic peoples, and others—were also dehumanised and placed lower in this hierarchy. Supporters of the ideology were taught that history was a struggle between races rather than classes, nations, or ideas. This “racial struggle” was presented as unavoidable and constant. From this perspective, politics was not about compromise or pluralism, but about survival and dominance. Another key belief was Lebensraum (“living space”). Nazis argued that Germany needed territorial expansion, especially eastward into Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union, to secure land and resources for the German people. In their ideology, this expansion was framed not as conquest, but as a natural necessity for survival. This justified aggressive war and, in practice, led directly to invasion, occupation, and mass atrocities. Nazi ideology also rejected liberal democracy, parliamentary politics, and communism. Democracy was portrayed by supporters as weak, chaotic, and incapable of defending the nation’s interests. Communism was framed as both a political and racial threat, often tied in propaganda to antisemitic conspiracy theories. The Nazi vision instead centred on authoritarian leadership under the Führerprinzip—the idea that absolute authority should rest in a single leader whose will represented the nation. Economically and socially, the ideology promoted a highly controlled society that subordinated individual rights to the state and to the supposed needs of the “national community” (Volksgemeinschaft). Class conflict was meant to be replaced by national unity—though in reality, this “unity” was enforced through exclusion, persecution, and violence against those deemed outside the community. For supporters at the time, these ideas were reinforced through propaganda, mass rallies, youth organisations, and strict control of media and education. The ideology was presented as restoring pride after Germany’s defeat in World War I and the perceived humiliation of the Treaty of Versailles. Many supporters believed they were participating in a national rebirth. However, in practice, Nazi ideology drove some of the most destructive events in modern history. It underpinned the persecution and systematic murder of six million Jews in the Holocaust, alongside millions of other victims. It justified aggressive expansion that triggered World War II in Europe, leading to tens of millions of deaths, widespread destruction, and long-term political consequences across the globe. After 1945, the exposure of these outcomes led to near-universal condemnation of Nazi ideology and its classification as a form of genocidal, racist extremism.
